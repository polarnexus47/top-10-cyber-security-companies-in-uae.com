Threat Landscape 2026

Why UAE Organizations Can No Longer Afford Generic Cybersecurity

Four threat vectors define the 2026 landscape for UAE businesses.

Ransomware Triple Extortion

Groups now encrypt data, threaten public exposure and pressure the victim's clients at the same time. Finance, healthcare and logistics organisations in the UAE hold data that attackers can monetise in several ways, which makes them frequent targets. Recovery depends on tested backups and an incident-response plan, not on the ransom decision.

AI-Powered Phishing & Deepfakes

AI-generated phishing is more convincing and cheaper to produce than before, and it targets multi-factor authentication flows through fake login pages and push-approval fatigue. Voice and video impersonation of executives is a documented technique. Awareness training that has not been updated since 2023 does not cover it.

Supply Chain & Initial Access Brokers

Attackers compromise a trusted software vendor or contractor and move into the enterprise network with legitimate access, so perimeter controls do not trigger. Initial access brokers sell this foothold to other groups. Vendor access reviews and testing of third-party connections reduce the exposure.

Regulatory Acceleration

The UAE Information Assurance (IA) Regulation, historically known as NESA, applies to government entities and organisations designated as critical. The Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) requires appropriate technical and organisational security measures and breach notification. The Dubai Electronic Security Center (DESC) runs an accreditation scheme for penetration testing and incident response providers, a quality signal buyers can verify.

UAE Cybersecurity Strategy 2025–2031 targets a fully resilient national digital infrastructure. Organisations that delay building a mature security programme carry growing regulatory and commercial risk.

Banks and financial institutions

Banks buy a combination of continuous monitoring and periodic deep testing of web, mobile and API channels. The financial regulator you report to shapes the evidence you need: DFSA-regulated firms in DIFC, FSRA-regulated firms in ADGM and CBUAE-supervised banks all expect regular independent testing. Ask each provider which of these regulators it has worked with and request a sample report.

Telecom and critical infrastructure

Operators of critical infrastructure face targeted attacks and often fall under the UAE IA Regulation. They need providers with OT/ICS experience and scale. Providers with national-programme backing and telecom heritage are listed for this need in their cards. Confirm named references and the delivery team's location before signing.

Scale-ups and fast-growing businesses

A scale-up rarely needs a full managed SOC at the start. A well-scoped penetration test before a release, a clear retest policy and direct access to the specialists who do the work usually give more value. Some providers also deliver outside the UAE; confirm delivery scope with each provider.

UAE Cybersecurity Regulations Every Vendor Must Know

Choosing a provider without understanding UAE's regulatory landscape leads to overspend or regulatory penalties. Verify your vendor's knowledge of these frameworks before signing.

UAE Information Assurance Regulation (NESA)

The UAE Information Assurance (IA) Regulation was developed by the National Electronic Security Authority (NESA), now part of the Signals Intelligence Agency, and applies to government entities and organisations designated as critical. Providers unfamiliar with it cannot map deliverables to its controls. Confirm the current version and the administering authority for your sector.

DESC ISR

Dubai Electronic Security Center Information Security Regulation, relevant to Dubai government entities and organisations in scope of Dubai's information security framework. DESC maintains an accreditation scheme for penetration testing and incident response providers, and Dubai government procurement often asks for accredited providers. Check your own scope with DESC before assuming the regulation applies.

UAE PDPL

Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the protection of personal data. It requires appropriate technical and organisational measures to protect personal data (Article 20) and notification of personal data breaches (Article 9). Cross-border transfers are regulated rather than prohibited, and some sector rules and government contracts require local processing.

ISO 27001

A widely requested certification in UAE enterprise procurement. In the 2022 edition, management of technical vulnerabilities is control A.8.8, and the transition from the 2013 edition ended on 31 October 2025. Penetration testing is one way to validate that control. Source: iso.org

PCI DSS

Applies to organisations that process, store or transmit payment card data. In PCI DSS v4.x, Requirement 11.4 covers external and internal penetration testing at least once every 12 months and after significant changes, while Requirement 11.3 covers vulnerability scanning. Source: pcisecuritystandards.org

ADHICS & VARA

ADHICS is the Abu Dhabi Department of Health's information and cyber security standard for healthcare entities, and its controls cover vulnerability management and security testing. VARA is the Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority in Dubai; its rulebooks set technology and information security expectations for licensed virtual asset service providers, so confirm the current testing obligations in the rulebooks.

Financial and Virtual-Asset Regulators

Financial and virtual-asset firms answer to sector regulators in addition to the cyber rules above. The Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE) supervises banks and other licensed financial institutions at federal level. The Dubai Financial Services Authority (DFSA) regulates financial services in the Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC). The Financial Services Regulatory Authority (FSRA) does the same in Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM). The Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA) is the federal securities regulator and has a regime for crypto assets onshore. The Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (VARA) regulates virtual asset activity in Dubai outside DIFC.

DESC and the UAE IA Regulation address information security for government and critical sectors; they are not crypto regulators. Where a regulator asks for regular independent security testing, confirm the exact wording in its current rulebook before you plan the scope.

DIFC Data Protection Law applies to entities operating within the Dubai International Financial Centre. Financial firms based in DIFC face dual compliance obligations under both DIFC DP and UAE PDPL. Verify your provider understands both frameworks if you operate in DIFC.

Enterprise vs SMB: Which Provider Type Do You Need?

Selecting based on name recognition rather than fit is the most common procurement mistake in UAE cybersecurity.

Enterprise (500+ employees)

Large enterprises need a combination of ongoing managed security (SOC/MDR) and periodic deep-dive assessments. Help AG, CPX, and Etisalat Digital are built for this model — offering 24/7 coverage, SLA-backed response times, and compliance reporting. For consolidated platforms, Palo Alto Networks and Trend Micro lead.

Mid-Market (50–500 employees)

Mid-market firms rarely need a full managed SOC. They need periodic penetration testing before audits or product releases, compliance-aligned reporting, and optionally a virtual CISO (vCISO). Boutique firms including Paranoid Security serve this segment well with deep manual testing and direct specialist access.

Fintech & Crypto

This segment requires manual testing of business logic and API endpoints (not just automated scanning), compliance reporting for PCI DSS and UAE PDPL, and in some cases crypto wallet forensics capability. Paranoid Security's focus on this vertical — with manual-first methodology and dedicated crypto forensics — is the strongest technical match.

Government & Critical Infrastructure

Procurement from DESC-accredited and locally-sovereign providers is typically required or strongly preferred. Help AG and CPX are the primary choices, with Injazat for cloud-integrated government environments requiring unified IT and security delivery.

Penetration Testing Vendor Selection: The 6-Question Checklist

If the primary service you need is penetration testing — not ongoing managed security — use this checklist when evaluating providers.

  1. What evidence of tester competence does the provider show? Certifications where held, original vulnerability research, CVE publications and sample reports all count. Ask about the testers who will work on your engagement, not the firm's general staff count.
  2. Is the test manual, automated, or hybrid? Automated vulnerability scanning finds known CVEs. Manual penetration testing finds business logic flaws, chained attack paths, and authentication bypasses that no scanner detects. For financial applications and APIs, manual testing is not optional.
  3. Do they offer PTaaS (Penetration Testing as a Service)? If your application releases frequently, a once-per-year test is insufficient. PTaaS provides continuous testing coverage with a persistent dashboard showing open findings and remediation progress.
  4. What does the deliverable include? A high-quality report contains: CVSS-scored findings with proof-of-concept evidence, business risk context, step-by-step remediation guidance, and an executive summary. If a provider cannot show you a sample report, do not proceed.
  5. Is retesting included? After remediation, vulnerabilities must be retested to confirm fixes. Some providers charge separately for retesting — confirm this before signing.
  6. Do they map findings to your compliance framework? If you need to demonstrate NESA, PCI DSS, or ISO 27001 compliance, your report needs to map findings to the relevant controls. Ask providers whether this mapping is standard or requires additional work.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a penetration test cost in the UAE?

Editorial estimate for October 2026: a basic web application test starts around AED 7,000, and broad enterprise assessments covering web, mobile, internal network and external perimeter can reach AED 50,000 and above. Red team engagements are priced per project and run over several months.

Cost depends on scope, application complexity, and provider methodology. Boutique firms with senior-only teams typically charge more than automated-first providers, but the quality of findings differs significantly. A report full of automated CVEs is not equivalent to a manual test that finds exploitable business logic flaws.

How long does a penetration test take?

A web application penetration test typically takes 2–4 weeks from scoping to final report. Internal and external network penetration tests run 3–6 weeks depending on infrastructure size. Red team engagements are multi-month projects.

Any provider quoting less than two weeks for a comprehensive manual test of a complex application should be asked to explain their methodology in detail.

What certifications should I look for when hiring a cybersecurity firm in UAE?

Look for evidence, not a single credential. For penetration testing, ask for the individual testers' certifications where they hold them, original vulnerability research and sample reports. For compliance services, ISO 27001 lead auditor credentials and PCI QSA status matter. For managed security, ask about DESC accreditation where your work falls under Dubai government procurement. Firm-level badges matter less than the work and credentials of the people assigned to your engagement.

What is the difference between a penetration test and a vulnerability assessment?

A vulnerability assessment uses automated tools to identify known vulnerabilities — it produces a list of CVEs with severity ratings. A penetration test goes further: a human tester attempts to exploit identified vulnerabilities, chain multiple weaknesses together to demonstrate real attack paths, and finds logic flaws that automated tools cannot detect.

Compliance frameworks including PCI DSS and ISO 27001 specify penetration testing, not just vulnerability scanning. If a provider is offering a "penetration test" that is primarily automated, that does not satisfy these requirements.

What is NESA and does my company need to comply?

NESA is the former National Electronic Security Authority. The UAE Information Assurance (IA) Regulation it developed applies to government entities and organisations designated as critical infrastructure. If your organisation is neither, the regulation is not a direct obligation. Government and critical-sector clients often ask suppliers to align with it, so a provider should understand its controls. Check the current version and administering authority on uaecybersecurity.gov.ae.

What is MDR and do I need it?

Managed Detection and Response (MDR) is an outsourced security service where a provider monitors your environment 24/7, detects threats, and responds to incidents on your behalf. It is appropriate for organisations that cannot staff an internal SOC team but need continuous coverage.

Penetration testing and MDR serve different purposes — testing identifies where your vulnerabilities are; MDR monitors for active exploitation of those vulnerabilities. Mature security programmes use both.

How do I get a security assessment quote?

Most providers on this list publish contact details on their websites. For boutique specialists like Paranoid Security, direct outreach is recommended. For larger MSSPs such as Help AG, CPX, or Etisalat Digital, their websites include request forms and sales contacts tailored to enterprise engagements.

When requesting a quote, be prepared to describe: the type of test required, the number and type of applications or systems in scope, your compliance framework, and your timeline. Providers who ask these questions upfront are more likely to deliver accurate scoping.

Is a UAE-based provider legally required for PDPL compliance?

No general rule in the PDPL requires personal data to be processed only in the UAE. The law regulates cross-border transfers (Articles 22–23) and requires appropriate security measures (Article 20). Some sector rules and government contracts do require local processing. Ask any provider where logs and test data are stored, which sub-processors it uses and which contract clauses govern transfers.

Which UAE providers hold DESC accreditation?

DESC publishes the list of accredited providers, and the register is the only reliable source. In this ranking, the Help AG card lists DESC accreditation for penetration testing and incident response. Check the DESC register for the current status before you rely on it, and confirm that the accreditation covers the service you plan to buy.

How do I choose a provider for a fintech or crypto company in the UAE?

Start with your regulator: DFSA, FSRA, CBUAE, SCA or VARA, depending on where you are licensed. Then ask for manual testing of web, mobile and API channels, a sample report with proof-of-concept evidence, a retest policy, and, for crypto firms, blockchain forensics capability. Our guide for banks, fintech and crypto firms lists the questions to ask.

What should I do in the first hours after a ransomware attack?

Isolate affected systems from the network without switching them off, preserve logs and the ransom note, and activate your incident-response plan or retainer provider. Notify your regulator and the relevant UAE authorities where required, and avoid contacting the attackers before legal and specialist advice. The incident response guide has a 24-hour checklist.

Which regulators cover crypto firms in the UAE?

It depends on where the firm operates. VARA covers Dubai outside DIFC, DFSA covers DIFC, FSRA covers ADGM and SCA covers the federal level. A firm can face more than one regime, for example a Dubai-licensed exchange that also serves clients elsewhere. Confirm your licence perimeter with a lawyer, then confirm the security expectations in each relevant rulebook.
